For years, the advice for job seekers was simple: make your CV ATS-friendly. Use a clean layout,
standard section headings, and the right keywords, and the applicant tracking system will let you
through to a human.

That advice still holds, but it now covers only the first step. Many of the hiring platforms
employers use today also run AI resume screening. The AI reads your CV, compares it with the
requirements of the specific job, and tells the recruiter how well you match before a person has
looked at it closely. A CV that is easy to parse but does not show the right evidence for that
job ends up lower on the list.

This post explains what changed, which tools do it, and how to write a CV that holds up when an AI
reads it.

## The old rule: an ATS-friendly CV

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is the software that collects applications for a job. In its
classic form it does three things:

- **Parses** your CV into fields such as name, job titles, dates, education, and skills.
- **Stores and searches** applications, so a recruiter can look for candidates with specific
  terms such as "Python" or "account management".
- **Filters** on knockout questions, such as work authorization or a required certification.

Being ATS-friendly meant making sure the parser could read you and the search could find you.
Avoid tables and text in images, use headings like "Experience" and "Education", and include the
terms the job posting uses. If you got through, a recruiter would skim your CV and decide.

## What changed: AI now reads and scores your CV

The new layer works differently. Instead of waiting for a recruiter to search, the AI checks every
application against the job's requirements and gives the recruiter a ranked or scored list. These
are not niche tools. They are built into some of the most widely used hiring platforms:

- **Workday Recruiting (HiredScore).** Workday's HiredScore AI analyzes every resume against the
  job requirements and grades each candidate. It evaluates each qualification on its own, and
  recruiters see which required and preferred qualifications a candidate meets, which they do not,
  and a plain-language explanation for each. Recruiters can then filter by qualifications met.
- **LinkedIn Hiring Assistant.** LinkedIn's AI agent for recruiters turns the recruiter's hiring
  qualifications into searches, evaluates candidate profiles to assess how well they align with
  those qualifications, and ranks the results.
- **Ashby (AI-Assisted Application Review).** The recruiter defines criteria for the role, one
  skill or requirement per criterion. Ashby's AI assesses each resume against every criterion and
  returns "meets", "does not meet", or "undecided", along with a percentage of criteria met that
  the recruiter can sort and filter by.
- **Lever (Talent Fit).** Lever gives each candidate a percentage-based score that reflects how
  closely their skills align with the job description.

All four vendors say a person makes the final call: the AI grades, ranks, and explains, and
recruiters decide who moves forward. That is true, but think about what it means in practice. A
recruiter with three hundred applications starts with the candidates the AI put at the top and
filters on the qualifications it marked as met. If your CV does not show the evidence for a
requirement, the AI marks it as missing, and you may never reach the part of the list a person
reads.

## ATS screening vs. AI screening

The two layers check different things, and a good CV has to pass both:

- **What it looks for.** An ATS search looks for terms. AI screening looks for evidence that you
  meet each requirement, even when you describe it in different words.
- **What the recruiter sees.** An ATS shows a list of applications that match a search. AI
  screening shows a grade, score, or criterion-by-criterion verdict for every applicant.
- **What it rewards.** An ATS rewards a readable format and the right keywords. AI screening
  rewards clear, specific proof that lines up with what this job asks for.
- **What it punishes.** An ATS punishes CVs it cannot parse. AI screening punishes CVs where the
  relevant experience is missing, vague, or buried under unrelated detail.

An ATS-friendly format is still the entry ticket. If the system cannot read your CV, nothing else
matters. But format alone no longer decides where you land.

## Why one generic CV now costs you interviews

The key change is that AI screening is done per job. Ashby's criteria, Workday's required and
preferred qualifications, and Lever's alignment score are all calculated against one specific
posting. The same CV gets a different result for every job it is sent to.

A generic CV is written to cover everything you have done. That makes it a weak match for any
single role:

- **The evidence is there, but in the wrong place.** Your strongest proof for this job is the
  fourth bullet of a role from five years ago, described in general terms.
- **The words do not line up.** The posting asks for "stakeholder management" and your CV says
  "worked with other teams". A modern AI can often connect the two, but a clear, direct statement
  is always easier to match than an inference.
- **Unrelated detail dilutes the match.** Half the CV describes work this employer did not ask
  about, so the parts that matter are harder to find for the AI and for the recruiter after it.

A tailored CV fixes all three. It is the same person and the same true history, arranged so that
every requirement the employer listed has visible evidence next to it.

## How to write a CV that holds up to AI screening

You do not need to trick the AI. You need to make the match easy to see.

1. **List the posting's requirements first.** Before editing anything, write down the skills,
   tools, experience, and responsibilities the job asks for, and note which are required and
   which are preferred. This is roughly the list the AI will check you against.
2. **Give each requirement clear evidence.** For every requirement you meet, make sure one bullet
   or line in your CV shows it directly: what you did, where, and with what result.
3. **Use the posting's terms where they are accurate.** If you did the work, describe it with the
   employer's words. This helps keyword search, AI matching, and the recruiter skimming afterwards.
4. **Be specific and measurable.** "Reduced onboarding time from three weeks to one" is easier to
   recognize as proof than "improved onboarding processes".
5. **Put the most relevant experience first.** Reorder bullets and skills so the strongest matches
   for this job appear at the top of each section.
6. **Keep the format simple.** Standard headings, a single column, real text rather than images.
   The ATS rules still apply.
7. **Do not stuff keywords or claim what you cannot back up.** Repeating terms without evidence
   does not satisfy a criterion that asks for proof, and anything you cannot explain will come up
   at the interview.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do companies use AI to screen resumes?

Yes. Workday Recruiting, LinkedIn Recruiter, Ashby, and Lever all offer AI features that compare
candidates with a job's requirements and grade, score, or rank them for the recruiter. Whether a
given employer turns these features on varies, so it is safest to assume your CV may be read by
an AI first.

### Is an ATS-friendly CV still necessary?

Yes. The AI can only evaluate what the system was able to read from your CV, so a clean,
parseable format is still the foundation. It is just no longer enough on its own.

### Does AI resume screening only look for keywords?

No. Tools such as Ashby's and Workday's evaluate whether you meet each requirement and explain the
result, which goes beyond counting matching words. Using the posting's terms still helps, but
specific evidence matters more than repetition.

### Should I send a different CV for every job?

Send a tailored version of the same CV. Your history does not change, but which parts you put
first, how you phrase them, and what you leave out should change with each posting, because the
screening is done against that posting.
